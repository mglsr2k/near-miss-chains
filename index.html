Near-miss chains

Most near misses end because one link in the chain held.

A tired crew, a misheard clearance, a busy descent: accidents rarely have one cause. This agent reads thousands of pilots’ own near-miss reports and NTSB accident investigations to find the chains of small errors that keep recurring, what caught them in the near misses, and how the same chains ended when nothing did.

Who it’s forSafety analysts, flight-safety officers, student and working pilots, and researchers who want evidence, not generic advice.

How to use itAsk about an error, condition, event or phase of flight. Watch the researchers work on the scope, then click any citation to read the report.

What it won’t doRank airlines or measure risk and trends. The reports are voluntary and have no flight-hour totals, and the agent will say so.

Where the evidence comes from

Two public datasets, one per team member, copied offline and tagged with one shared vocabulary of factors and events. There is no ID linking a near miss to an accident; the shared tags are the bridge.

NASA Aviation Safety Reporting System

14,131

Voluntary, de-identified near-miss reports by pilots, controllers, mechanics and cabin crew, with a narrative in their own words and expert-coded fields, including who detected the problem and what was done. Mostly airline flying, almost all from mid-2024 on. Source: asrs.arc.nasa.gov.

NTSB aviation accident database

31,453

Investigated accidents and incidents since 2008, with probable cause, coded findings, the defining event and injuries. Mostly general aviation. Source: data.ntsb.gov/avdata.

How a question is researched

TriageAsks you to narrow a vague question, or explains what the data can’t answer.
LeadSplits the question and sends two to four researchers at once.
ResearchersEach queries both datasets in a fresh context, then reports back with report IDs.
Lead againSends more researchers if a gap remains, up to a cap.
Writer + checkWrites the cited report; code verifies every cited ID came from a tool.

What this data can’t tell you

  • Rates, risk or trends. ASRS is voluntary, so counts reflect who chose to report, and neither dataset has flight hours.
  • Rankings of airlines, airports or aircraft. ASRS hides names, and nothing is adjusted for how much each one flies.
  • A near-miss-to-accident ratio. The two datasets cover different kinds of flying over different years.
  • Events before 2008 (NTSB) or mostly before mid-2024 (ASRS), military flights, or non-US events.
